## Summary

Chemical bonding, covered as a comparison rather than a list. Ionic and
covalent bonding were introduced through the electronegativity difference
between the two atoms, with the lecturer stressing that the cutoff at 1.7 is a
convention and not a law — most bonds sit somewhere on a continuum and are
described as polar covalent. Lattice energy was introduced for ionic solids and
used to explain why NaCl melts at 801 °C while water melts at 0 °C. The last
fifteen minutes went to hydrogen bonding, and to the standard example of why
ice floats.

## Key Points

- **Electronegativity difference decides the character**
  - $\Delta \chi > 1.7$ — largely ionic
  - $0.4 < \Delta \chi < 1.7$ — polar covalent
  - $\Delta \chi < 0.4$ — essentially non-polar covalent
  - The boundaries are conventions; bonding is a continuum

- **Ionic bonding**
  - Electron transferred, not shared; a cation and an anion held by
    electrostatic attraction
  - Forms a lattice, not a molecule — there is no "NaCl molecule" in a crystal
  - Lattice energy rises with charge and falls with ionic radius

- **Covalent bonding**
  - Electron pair shared between two nuclei
  - Discrete molecules with weak forces between them, hence low melting points

- **Why NaCl melts at 801 °C and H₂O at 0 °C**
  - Melting NaCl means breaking the whole lattice
  - Melting ice means breaking hydrogen bonds between intact molecules, which
    is far cheaper

- **Hydrogen bonding**
  - H attached to N, O or F, attracted to a lone pair on a neighbouring N, O or F
  - Roughly 5–30 kJ/mol — much stronger than van der Waals, much weaker than covalent
  - Ice is less dense than liquid water because the hydrogen-bonded lattice
    holds the molecules further apart than they sit when jostling in the liquid

## Questions

**1. What decides whether a bond is ionic or covalent?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The electronegativity difference $\Delta\chi$ between the two atoms. Above
about 1.7 the bond is described as largely ionic; between 0.4 and 1.7 as polar
covalent; below 0.4 as essentially non-polar covalent. These cutoffs are
conventions — real bonding is a continuum with ionic and covalent as the two
extremes.

</details>

**2. Why does sodium chloride melt at 801 °C while ice melts at 0 °C?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

NaCl is an ionic lattice: melting it requires breaking the electrostatic
attractions holding the entire three-dimensional array together.

Ice is a molecular solid: melting it only requires breaking hydrogen bonds
*between* water molecules, while the covalent O–H bonds within each molecule
stay intact. Hydrogen bonds are roughly 5–30 kJ/mol against several hundred for
the lattice, so far less energy is needed.

</details>

**3. What conditions are needed for a hydrogen bond?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a strongly electronegative atom (N, O or
F), and a lone pair on a nearby N, O or F for that hydrogen to be attracted to.
The small size and high electronegativity of these three atoms is why the list
is so short.

</details>

**4. Explain why ice is less dense than liquid water.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In ice each water molecule is hydrogen-bonded to four others in a fixed
tetrahedral arrangement, which is an open structure with space inside it. In
liquid water the hydrogen bonds are constantly breaking and reforming, letting
molecules pack more closely on average. More space per molecule means lower
density, so ice floats.

</details>
