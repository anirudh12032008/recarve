## Threads

**Everything in this lecture came back to one question: what has to break?**
Melting point, solubility and hardness were each explained the same way — by
asking which forces a change of state actually destroys. Ionic solids lose a
lattice, molecular solids lose only the forces between molecules. Expect this
reasoning to be the expected answer whenever a comparison of physical
properties is asked for.

**Conventions are not laws.** The 1.7 cutoff was flagged twice as a
convenience. A question asking you to classify a borderline bond is usually
asking you to say that it is intermediate, not to pick a side.

## Likely questions

**1. Compare ionic and covalent bonding under four headings: electron behaviour, structure, melting point and electrical conductivity.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

| | Ionic | Covalent |
|---|---|---|
| Electrons | Transferred | Shared |
| Structure | Extended lattice, no discrete molecules | Discrete molecules |
| Melting point | High — the lattice must break | Low — only intermolecular forces break |
| Conductivity | Conducts when molten or dissolved, not as a solid | Generally does not conduct |

</details>

**2. Why is hydrogen bonding restricted to H attached to N, O or F?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Those three are small and strongly electronegative, so the H–X bond is very
polar and the hydrogen carries a large partial positive charge on a nucleus
with almost no shielding electrons. That concentrated charge can approach a
lone pair closely. Larger or less electronegative atoms give neither the
polarity nor the close approach.

</details>

**3. Arrange NaCl, H₂O and CH₄ in order of increasing melting point and justify the order.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

CH₄ < H₂O < NaCl.

CH₄ is non-polar with only weak dispersion forces between molecules. H₂O is
molecular too, but hydrogen-bonded, so more energy is needed. NaCl is an ionic
lattice and requires far more energy than either.

</details>
