## Threads

**One number carried the whole lecture.** Chain length, pyramid shape and the
land-efficiency argument were all consequences of the ten percent law rather
than separate facts. If an exam question asks "why" about any structural
feature of an ecosystem, the loss of energy between levels is very likely the
answer it wants.

**Cycles versus flows.** Energy passes through an ecosystem once and leaves as
heat; matter goes round. The carbon cycle was introduced specifically to make
that contrast, and the fossil fuel problem was framed as reintroducing carbon
that the cycle had taken out of circulation.

## Likely questions

**1. Explain the ten percent law and its consequences for ecosystem structure.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

About 10% of the energy at one trophic level reaches the next; the rest is lost
mainly as heat through respiration.

Consequences: food chains are limited to roughly 4–5 levels because too little
energy remains beyond that; pyramids of energy are always upright; and food
production is more efficient at lower trophic levels.

</details>

**2. Distinguish between a pyramid of numbers and a pyramid of energy.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A pyramid of numbers counts individual organisms per level and can be inverted
— one tree supporting thousands of insects.

A pyramid of energy measures energy flow per level and is always upright,
because energy is lost at every transfer and no level can hold more than the
one beneath it.

</details>

**3. Outline the carbon cycle and say where human activity disturbs it.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Photosynthesis fixes atmospheric CO₂ into organic matter; respiration and
decomposition return it to the atmosphere; some carbon is stored long-term in
sediments and fossil fuels.

Human activity disturbs the cycle mainly by burning fossil fuels, which returns
carbon locked away for millions of years far faster than natural processes
remove it, and by deforestation, which reduces the rate of fixation.

</details>
